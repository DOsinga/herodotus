  Chile extends along South America's Pacific coast between the Andes and the ocean. This record begins with independence from Spain in 1818 and follows the state through later changes of government. Santiago became the center of a republic whose institutions developed amid conflicts between conservative and liberal leaders. Nineteenth-century expansion incorporated disputed southern territories and northern lands gained in the War of the Pacific. Mining, especially copper, became central to the economy. Salvador Allende's socialist presidency ended in the military coup of 1973, followed by Augusto Pinochet's dictatorship and extensive repression. Civilian democratic government returned in 1990. Chile retains a presidential republican system, while disputes over inequality, Indigenous rights, and constitutional reform reflect unresolved questions about its national political settlement.
