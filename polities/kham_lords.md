description: >-
  After the Tibetan Empire broke apart in the civil wars that followed the death of Langdarma
  in 842, no king governed Kham, the eastern of the three Tibetan regions, between the upper
  Yangtze, Mekong and Salween and the Sichuan border. Its valleys and grasslands were ruled by
  local chieftains, clan houses and, increasingly, by monasteries, in a patchwork whose borders
  shifted with marriage alliances, feuds and the patronage of religious teachers. Some of these
  lordships, such as Nangchen in the north and Chakla at Dartsedo, later became lasting
  kingdoms. Mongol armies imposed their authority in the 1250s. When Yuan rule lapsed in the
  1360s the region fell back to its local lords until monastic and royal states took hold
  again.
